An experiment, not an animation. An electron wave heads toward a proton, and the computer only solves the equations of physics: nothing is rolled, nothing is forced together, and no orbital shapes or energy levels are put in. Whatever forms comes out of the equations. Then we compare it with what is known and ask what is missing.

    What is in the equations, and what is left out

    In: the Coulomb pull; the proton's motion (exact for two bodies); the electron's spin with spin–orbit coupling, the relativistic speed correction and the Darwin term; and quantized light in free space, at first order in its coupling, so a photon can grow out of the field's vacuum while the electron drops into a bound shape. After the collision the captured electron cascades down by the same coupling.

    Left out: the Lamb shift (0.000004 eV; it needs loop corrections of quantum electrodynamics), the proton's own magnetism (hyperfine, the 21 cm line, which takes millions of years), the 2s state's two-photon decay (0.12 s), gravity (10⁻³⁹ of the pull), the proton's size and the nuclear forces (about 10⁻¹⁰), and pair creation (a million times more energy). Light given off by an electron that stays free (bremsstrahlung) is not counted yet.

    How it is solved: the wave is fully 3D, on spheres around the proton: 81 angular patterns times radial points 0.05 Bohr radii apart near the proton (0.2 far out), out to 17 nm, where the edge absorbs whatever reaches it. Each step is 2.4 attoseconds. The 3D view shows the cloud as glowing gas: a pixel's brightness is how much electron lies along that line of sight, inside a sphere around the proton whose size is the zoom. The wave is the same all the way round the line it came in on, so the computer stores one half-slice through that line and the view turns it round. A run is exactly repeatable: the same settings always give the same numbers, to the last digit, on any computer.
